Hi Julia, nice to meet you! Can you tell us something about yourself?
My name is Julia Meerman and I am 25 years old. After I finished my Masters in Biomedical Sciences in Nijmegen, I joined the RIVM and IRAS as a PhD student, within the VHP4Safety Project. For a very long time I thought that I didn't want to do a PhD, but as I was doing my Master internships I realized I genuinely wanted to continue doing hands-on research. A PhD seemed like the best way for me to do that.


Within the project I am working in work package 2.3, which focuses on Parkinson's disease and pesticides. I am the person who works on delineating the mechanisms behind Parkinson's disease and how pesticides can lead to neurodegeneration. I am also responsible for generating experimental data within our work package.


What interested you the most about the VHP4Safety project?
There are two main things that really drew me to this project. The first is the development and use of in silico methods for risk assessment. The second reason has more to do with the necessity of risk and safety assessment based on non-animal testing. During my internships, I worked a lot within the field of reproductive toxicology, focusing specifically on whether drugs can cross and/or affect the placenta, and I was amazed at how little we actually still know about some compounds. It is very similar for neurodegeneration. That is bizarre when you think about it, because on a daily bases we come into contact - consciously and unconsciously - with so many compounds. Because of this lack of knowledge, I think it is very important that we do something about this and come up with better methods to investigate the effects of compounds on our health and in, particular, on neurodegeneration.


Recently, you published your first paper. Congratulations! What’s it about? Can you give us a brief summary?
Thank you! The starting point of this paper was a recently released RIVM report. They assessed the extent to which currently required tests for risk and safety assessments of active substances in plant protection products can say something about their effects on neurodegeneration. The outcome was essentially that these tests are not adequate to draw conclusions about any possible effects on neurodegeneration. Although there are specific neurotoxicity tests, they only focus on acute effects, they do not include markers for specific neurodegenerative disorders, and they are not routinely required. As such, these specific neurotoxicity tests also fall short.

In our paper we then considered what is known from a scientific perspective; what is known about the link between pesticide exposure and neurodegeneration. We try to reflect the problem that we see; that this link exist, but that we currently cannot capture neurodegenerative effects in risk and safety assessment. We then provide short- and long-term suggestions for how we can solve this. Because it is a serious problem.


And what are those suggestions?
For the short term, we think it would be an improvement to add parameters specific for neurodegeneration in already existing animal tests - that is, animal tests that are going to be done in any case - so that you would have a small indication of what is happening to the brain without requiring additional animal tests to be performed. However, an animal experiment is not fully representative for the human situation. That is why we suggest longer-term solutions that are based on human biology. This is, for instance, the approach that VHP4Safety also has, in which we first outline the human biology underlying an adverse effect and develop a kind of test battery on that foundation. This will eventually allow us to do a better assessment of these pesticides.


The title of your paper is ‘neurodegeneration in a regulatory context: the need for speed’, but why is there need for speed?
We see a very big problem with, possibly, very serious consequences; we know from epidemiological studies that people who are exposed to pesticides may have an increased risk of developing neurodegenerative diseases, but we do not have regulatory measures and guidelines in place assessing the hazards a pesticide may pose. On the one hand we have a large group of chemicals being used, for which we do not know whether they are safe or not. That group is growing, as new pesticides are being brought onto the market. Because pesticides may persist in the environment even after they are banned, it is important to prevent pesticides that affect the brain being used as soon as possible.

On the other hand, we also have to take into account the delayed effect of pesticides, as neurodegenerative disorders develop over a long period of time. Any action we do not take now will have consequences for the coming decades.


What else is in store for you? Are you planning to publish more papers?
One of the tasks that I am currently working on is making an overview of the mechanisms involved in development of Parkinson’s disease, when it is related to pesticide exposure. Because we want to use this information to eventually develop a battery of tests, we are using the Adverse Outcome Pathway framework to structure the mechanisms involved. Using this framework, we determine the sequence of molecular and cellular events required to produce a toxic effect when an organism is exposed to a substance. Right now, I am working on a paper of this Adverse Outcome Pathway.


And then a word about yourself, what do you do when you're not busy with your research?
Korfball! I have actually played korfball my entire life. I play at quite a serious level, because I like the challenge it gives me. I also think it gives a larger sense of satisfaction when you win a difficult match. Playing korfball really helps me to unwind and think about something other than my research. During my studies I had practice in Nijmegen and now in Utrecht. During the weekends I still go back to my home-team in Zeeland for games. So you could say that I travel a lot in my spare time as well.
