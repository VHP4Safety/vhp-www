Hi Damiën! Can you tell us a little bit about yourself?
Sure! My name is Damiën van Berlo and I am involved in the VHP4Safety project as the lead of the risk assessment task force, which has been implemented within the VHP4Safety project to connect all things related to risk assessment. My background is primarily in toxicology, but I am broadly orientated within the biological/medical sciences. In the backpack of my career I carry three things that I think are particularly helpful for my current role in VHP4Safety: 1) in my previous job I worked on validation and implementation of innovative in vitro models, so I have some idea about the state-of-the-art regarding NAMs (New Approach Methodologies); 2) I have been involved in many different types of in vitro and in vivo studies (as a researcher and as Study Director at a Contract Research Lab) and 3) in my current job I work as a regulator/risk assessor so I have some insight regarding what is needed on the regulatory side of things to replace the animal test.

I currently work at the RIVM for Bureau REACH (which stands for Registration, Evaluation, Authorisation and Restriction of Chemicals), where I am a policy advisor on regulatory toxicology of chemicals. Here, I am involved primarily in the E in REACH: Evaluation, this refers to hazard and risk assessment.

How does the taskforce risk assessment operate? I can imagine it is a challenge to connect all things related to risk assessment within VHP4Safety.
Well, the ambition of VHP4Safety is of course sky high and I don’t think we should expect to achieve it all within the duration of the current project. In my estimation, it will take at least another decade to achieve the ultimate goal: better risk assessment without the use of animals. This means that for now, we have to narrow down a bit and focus on a limited number of case studies. There are currently three of those, one is about kidney disease, one is about pesticide-induced neurodegenerative disease and one is about thyroid effects in relation to age and gender. Each of those case studies has its own regulatory lead on it as well. One of the things that is not challenging is working with the people involved in the task force; honestly, it’s a joy rather than a chore. So it’s not as challenging as it could be 😉.

My role is to bring those studies and people together and, in a joint effort, harmonize the work performed: I try to be a facilitator in this process. We want to avoid invention of similar-but-different wheels by multiple project participants simultaneously; it would be less efficient and in the end the wheels should all fit on the same vehicle (symbolizing VHP4Safety).

What interested you the most about the VHP4Safety project?
The first thing would be an obvious one: the opportunity to work together with some of the top people in their fields. Then, from my perspective, a unique strength of this project compared to other similar projects is that regulators and risk assessors are directly and strongly involved. In VHP4Safety we have the opportunity to significantly participate and I believe that this is incredibly valuable. Ultimately, the risk assessors are also end users of VHP4Safety and the regulators know what is required in international regulations, so in my opinion it is crucial to include them in the process.

That also means that we, as regulators, should try to contribute in a constructive way. We are often known as people who are rather conservative and tend to step on the brake pedal a lot. This can be frustrating for academics, who are innovative by nature and want to push forward towards the new and better. Part of that has to do with our responsibility for public health and the environment; this is deeply rooted in how we think and act. If we would accept a new system that would eventually lead to damage to public health (or great damage to ecosystems), a lot of the blame would be put on our desks. Braking is better than breaking; but you cannot be on the brake pedal all the time, otherwise we would get nowhere but exactly where we already are. And where’s the fun in that? I think we should do our best to explain our point of view, so that other people at least understand our reasons, even if they might not agree with them. Similarly, we should listen closely to what other project partners and stakeholders have to say. There is plenty of common ground, I am convinced of that.

What do you notice in the conversations you have within the project?
Maybe the main thing is that we all want the same, we just do not always realize that. Some of us may talk about creating a triangle, others may talk about a square instead; this may seem very different. But actually, it is a pyramid we’re trying to build, we just look at it from a different viewpoint. There are many different disciplines represented within VHP4Safety; each with its own jargon. We often misunderstand each other. That is why it is important to keep the conversation going. Keep asking questions, keep trying to explain things in such a way that it is understandable for someone with a different background.

When is VHP4Safety a success for you?
I would consider it a great success when we achieve a shared vision with all the different stakeholders in VHP4Safety; “this is what we all believe and what we want to go for”. It doesn't even have to be very extensive or detailed. It would also be a success to achieve a good mutual understanding of what drives each of us; what is important and why? And more concretely, I would consider it a success to have the three case studies answer a specific regulatory question at the end of the project.

On a completely different note, the end of the year is now upon us; do you have any New Year's resolutions?
One that keeps coming back is to exercise more often, but I also want to find a better balance this year between working at the office and working from home. I notice that I still choose to work at home too often because it is convenient, while I actually get a lot of energy from the contact with my colleagues at the office.

What else energizes you?
Social events: being around people, having interesting or funny conversations. I like to take long walks and enjoy nature. I also enjoy doing sports and while everyone in the office goes for endurance sports, my cup of tea is powerlifting. In the summer holiday, I enjoy camping in France, read a book in front of our tent, drink some coffee or a nice beer while the kids enjoy themselves. And well, as contradicting as it sounds for a nature person, I really enjoy fast things on wheels (they can be electric!)… maybe this is to counteract the slow pace of the regulatory field 😉

Finally, is there anything you would like to say?
I really see the strong involvement of regulators within this project as a game-changer in itself. People who want to change the game often have their eyes on the distant horizon, but to get there you better look at your feet as well, at the small things that affect how and when you get there. We should not become frustrated if the major goals are a bit more distant than we hoped: maybe the path is the goal, because so much is learned on the journey itself. So I hope that the smaller, unique things about this project will be appreciated by my fellow project participants as well.
