Hi Elena! Could you tell us a bit about yourself?
Elena is a postdoctoral researcher in Work Package 2.2, where she contributes to the kidney case study. Originally from Mallorca, she realised early in her studies that she would need to move abroad to build an academic career. “Mallorca has only one university for five islands, so it became clear very quickly that if I wanted to make a career, I had to leave,” she explains.

After completing her bachelor’s degree in Madrid, she looked for opportunities elsewhere in Europe. “I wanted to live in a place where English is spoken outside the classroom. That left me with two options, the UK and the Netherlands, and the UK was a bit too expensive.” She moved to Maastricht, expecting the weather in the south of the Netherlands to be a bit milder. “But I was very wrong, it was actually cold and humid,” she laughs.

In Maastricht she took advantage of a Dutch rule that allows students to enrol in multiple master’s programmes for the standard tuition fee. She completed both Biomedical Sciences and the Research Master in Health Sciences. During her final year she did an internship at the UMC Utrecht and decided she wanted to stay in Utrecht. That internship led her, via via, to a PhD position, where she developed advanced kidney organoid platforms, originally aimed at gene therapy research.

Toward the end of her PhD she was unsure what direction to take next. “When Anne Metje was promoted to assistant professor and the postdoc position in VHP4Safety became available, it felt like a natural fit. The work builds on what I did during my PhD, but now we apply the same models to drug safety instead of gene therapy.”

You work in work package 2.2, the kidney case study. What do you study in this work package?
Within VHP4Safety, Elena studies nephrotoxic drugs. “I worked on tacrolimus and gentamicin,” she says. Gentamicin is not yet part of a regulatory question but will be added soon. She credits Fabian for helping translate in vitro work into platform-ready components. “His help in putting the case study information on the platform is invaluable. He’s great at building a bridge between the in vitro scientists and the platform developers.”

Why did you decide to also study gentamicin?
Gentamicin is a potent antibiotic with well-known kidney toxicity, especially relevant in neonates. “Our kidney organoid models, despite being described as advanced, resemble foetal kidneys more than adult ones,” she explains. “That makes them ideal for studying gentamicin toxicity in children.” A collaboration with the children’s hospital strengthened this direction: “They were very enthusiastic and even shared their clinical data from neonates treated with gentamicin. It’s becoming a really nice example of how combining various data sources can support a regulatory question.”

Understanding mechanisms to mitigate toxicity

Elena’s work aims to uncover mechanisms of toxicity, so that adverse outcomes can be mitigated. “Tacrolimus is essential for solid organ transplantation, but it’s very toxic for the kidney. If we know the adverse outcome pathway, we can identify key events where we might intervene.”
She notes that she cannot say as much about cisplatin, as her colleague Devon worked on that compound.

What challenges do you encounter in WP2.2?
Modelling the kidney in vitro remains a challenge. “Kidney models are less mature than those for other organs, and clinically the only readout we have is glomerular filtration rate. That makes it almost impossible to directly compare our in vitro results to clinical data.” Stronger clinical measurements or more advanced organoid models would help close that gap.

You also collaborate with the UMC Utrecht in this work package. How does your work complement each other?
Elena collaborates closely with Kate, who works at the Regenerative Medicine Center Utrecht. “We study the same compounds with different model systems. I use organoids derived from induced pluripotent stem cells. Kate uses patient-derived stem cells, which are much closer to the clinic.” Together they investigate tacrolimus effects on different parts of the nephron. “I look at the proximal part, Kate looks at the distal part, which is hardly studied, so that’s all very novel. Even when we see not a lot happening, negative results are also results.”

During the hackathon last September, we focused on putting the kidney case study on the platform. How do you see the impact of this platform, and having the kidney case study on it?
During the September hackathon the team worked on integrating the kidney case study into the VHP4Safety platform. Elena sees real value in this. “Building this platform is ambitious, but important. Lab work often stays in the lab. This consortium makes our work meaningful beyond publishing.” For the kidney case study, it also highlights what is still missing for future implementation. “The models need to become more mature, and as long as the clinic only measures the glomerular filtration rate, bridging the gap will remain difficult.”

From your perspective, what role can the three case studies play in the broader vision of the platform?
Elena emphasises that the value of the case studies goes beyond answering specific regulatory questions. “The most important thing is the process. How we built the platforms, how we investigated the mechanisms, the steps we took. Anyone working on gentamicin-like antibiotics or tacrolimus-class compounds can benefit from these examples, even if not in a one-to-one way.”

You are currently the interim work package leader. What does this role involve for you, and how have you experienced taking it on?
She is currently interim work package leader, a role she has found instructive. “It’s been a great opportunity to learn how a large project is organised. I’ve interacted with many other work packages, which gave me a better understanding of why timely data sharing matters. When we don’t deliver our data, others can’t move forward with building their tools.”

How has working in a large, interdisciplinary consortium influenced your research?
The interdisciplinary nature of the project has shaped her perspective. “You become more aware of the broader impact of your research. Many groups depend on your results. And you notice how much jargon you use. It’s important to develop a shared language.”

Your postdoc contract is coming to an end soon. Do you already have ideas or plans for what you would like to do next?
Elena is preparing for a full career switch and will move into patent and IP law. “I’m starting a four to five year traineeship in January 2027. In the meantime, from mid-February, I’ll be working at Utrecht Holdings as a patent and technology officer.”

And on a more personal note, what do you enjoy doing in your free time?
In her free time, she enjoys a mix of creative hobbies. “In winter I basically hibernate and watch Grey’s Anatomy,” she laughs. “The rest of the year, my hobby is to look for new hobbies. I’ve tried painting, pottery, crochet… But music is the one that stayed. I play guitar, bass and drums, and recently got into producing electronic music.” She is also a plant lover and visits Intratuin almost every Saturday. “And I got a sourdough starter recently, so that may become the next hobby.”

And of course, the Christmas season is on its way! Do you have any nice plans for the holidays?
For the Christmas break she will celebrate with her fiancée’s family in the Netherlands, and then travel to Mallorca at the end of December to catch some sun to survive the remaining months of the Dutch winter.
