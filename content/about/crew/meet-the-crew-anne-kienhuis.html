Hi Anne! Can you tell us a bit about yourself?

Anne Kienhuis has built a diverse career in health sciences, molecular sciences, and toxicogenomics. After completing her studies and a master’s degree at Maastricht University, she pursued a PhD in toxicogenomics at Maastricht University and TNO, focusing on developing an in vitro model for hepatotoxicity.

"During my PhD, I compared gene expression profiles between rat hepatocytes in vitro and in vivo models, and isolated human hepatocytes from tissue obtained from hospitals. My goal was to evaluate how well these models could predict hepatotoxicity after exposure to chemicals," she explains.

Her career later shifted towards bridging science and society.

"I did a postdoc at The Arts and Genomics Centre at Leiden University exploring how art can help engage the public in technological developments in life sciences. Art can play an important role in communicating scientific research," she adds.

Anne then joined RIVM, focusing on in vivo and in vitro hepatotoxicity models in mice and rats, toxicogenomics, and the zebrafish model. Eventually, she became the project coordinator for alternatives to animal testing at RIVM and the RIVM representative for the national TPI program of the government.

"Through this work, we developed the roadmap for animal-free innovations in the regulatory field, which laid the foundation for the Virtual Human Platform (VHP) project," Anne shares.

What inspired you to initiate the VHP4Safety project, and what drives your commitment to this work?

Anne’s passion for innovative safety assessments grew during her work on the Roadmap for animal-free innovations.

"During the multi-stakeholder discussions we had when developing our roadmap, we identified safety assessment of the future as one of the key themes, shifting from animal tests as the gold standard to a focus on human biology. It’s about using new technological innovations to better address regulatory needs," she explains.

This vision for advancing safety assessments and her belief in the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration are at the core of her commitment to the VHP4Safety project.

How did you approach writing the project proposal for VHP4Safety?

The idea for the Virtual Human Platform took shape during a 2018 brainstorming session between Anne and Cyrille Krul, where they discussed how to move towards the future of safety assessment.

"We saw the Virtual Human Platform as the ultimate alternative to the animal test. How do you create such a platform, and how do you integrate tools?" Anne recalls.

When the Dutch Research Agenda (NWA-ORC) announced a call for proposals, they took the opportunity to formalize their vision

In preparation of the research proposal, they teamed up with Juliette Legler as the overall coordinator of the Virtual Human Platform initiative. They involved toxicologists and experts from their networks to define the three research lines and organized workshops to refine the roles of the different work packages.

"We also agreed that social sciences should be an integral part of the project to help with the transition to our desired future," she adds.

The interdisciplinary research proposal on the Virtual Human Platform for Safety Assessment ultimately received funding.

What makes the VHP4Safety project unique in your eyes?

For Anne, the interdisciplinary nature of the project is what sets it apart.

"The consortium includes representatives from multiple disciplines and sectors. Risk assessors, scientific researchers, data scientists, and social scientists all play crucial roles," she explains.

The collaborative approach is reflected in the project’s innovative methodologies, such as Designathons and Hackathons, which encourage participants to work across disciplines.

Can you tell us more about your involvement in work package 3.2 and the key responsibilities it entails?

Anne leads work package 3.2, which focuses on the acceptance of the Virtual Human Platform and its building blocks.

"I’m particularly interested in the dynamics of technological ánd societal acceptance—how innovations become successful, both technically and institutionally," she explains. She collaborates with the Copernicus Institute, in which they also study this, and uses frameworks like the multi-level perspective and the NAMs implementation curve to study these processes.

Better data sharing can also help with the acceptance of new technologies, which is where the FAIR principles are applied.

The work package also includes exploring societal acceptance.

"We’re collaborating with an expert in risk communication to investigate how the public perceives new approaches to safety assessment and decision-making," Anne adds.

What are some challenges you’ve encountered in promoting acceptance of the Virtual Human Platform, and how have you addressed them?

Anne acknowledges the challenge of promoting acceptance for a platform that is still under development.

"The iterative nature of building the platform while simultaneously addressing its acceptance can be complex," she says.

The interdisciplinary collaboration within the project provides valuable insights into the complexity of acceptance and the importance of stakeholder involvement. Engaging with a diverse range of stakeholders, including experts from different disciplines, helps build broader support, but also brings new questions and challenges.

"We also collaborate with initiatives like ONTOX to stimulate broader acceptance in the toxicology community beyond our own consortium," Anne adds. This collaborative approach strengthens the platform’s credibility and fosters a wider understanding of its potential impact.

What are your aspirations for the impact of the Virtual Human Platform on safety assessments in the coming years?

Anne envisions the Virtual Human Platform as a transformative tool for safety assessments, with significant potential for collaboration and innovation.

"I see a lot of opportunities for collaboration with other initiatives, for example, in the integration of workflows," she explains. By connecting and streamlining different tools and approaches, the platform can improve efficiency and accuracy in safety assessments, ultimately enabling a more seamless process for both researchers and regulators.

Regulatory acceptance is another key focus for Anne.

"The impact of what we’re building depends on fostering trust and addressing new regulatory questions," she says. Ensuring that the platform and its building blocks meet the needs of regulators is critical for achieving widespread adoption and creating meaningful change in safety assessment practices.

Anne acknowledges that these are ambitious goals, but they are essential steps to fully realize the platform’s potential.

Outside of your professional work, what activities or hobbies do you enjoy?

Anne enjoys creative and artistic pursuits in her free time.

"The past weeks I’ve been busy with making Sinterklaas surprises! Next to this, I like sewing my own clothes, and I knit. I also play the flute in a chamber ensemble and love reading—I’m part of a book club," she shares.

Above all, she values spending quality time with her family.
