Hi Marc! Can you tell us a bit about yourself?

Marc has been working at Utrecht University of Applied Sciences for 21 years and lives in Utrecht with his wife and their six-year-old daughter. He has spent his entire professional life on the Uithof campus.

"I studied biology here, moved into student housing, and later conducted PhD research on brain-immune interactions at the Wilhelmina Children’s Hospital," he explains.

After finishing his PhD, Marc joined the University of Applied Sciences as a lecturer. However, after two years, he realized he missed research.

"That’s when I worked with Cyrille to establish a research group, which has evolved into the Innovative Testing research group, where I am now an Associate Professor," he says. The group focuses on alternatives to animal testing, a subject close to Marc’s heart, especially given his own experience with animal testing during his PhD.

“In the context of the project we’re working on, it should be possible to do it without animal testing, given the scientific advancements in the field,” he explains.

Outside of work, Marc is passionate about rock climbing, a hobby he’s been dedicated to for 30 years.

"I used to climb outdoors a lot, but these days I also do a lot of bouldering. It’s a great outlet for me," he adds.

Can you tell us more about your involvement in work package 1.1 and how you’re contributing to the development of the Virtual Human Platform?

In this project, Marc plays a creative and technical role, combining his knowledge of biology and toxicology with his expertise in computational models.

"I have a solid understanding of biological systems, toxicology, how to conduct studies on the effects of substances. On top of that, I know how to build and program computational models, and how to deploy them on a server," he says.

This combination of skills allows Marc to contribute to the platform’s design—not just regarding what it should do but also how to implement it in a user-friendly way. To help illustrate these ideas, he builds proofs of concept.

"For the Hackathon, for example, I created a prototype tool that I believe could become a functionality on the platform. I try to do this using the latest technologies," he notes.

Marc is also exploring new developments such as Large Language Models (LLMs) and how they can be applied within the platform.

"I want to challenge people to think about how these modern technologies can be integrated into our work," he explains.

Speaking of LLMs, you’ve recently started exploring the role of LLMs in the platform. What do you think are their biggest advantages and challenges?

Marc sees a lot of potential in using LLMs for scientific processes like knowledge synthesis.

"As scientists, we’re used to synthesizing knowledge through methods like evidence reviews and systematic reviews. With LLMs, we can do this much faster and on a larger scale," he explains.

He believes LLMs could help keep systematic reviews up-to-date, a challenge as they often become outdated by the time of publication. Another potential use of LLMs is as orchestrators, automating workflows by activating the right tools based on a specific input.

"For instance, if you input the SMILES code of a substance, the LLM could interpret which tool to activate in order to answer your query. This is much faster than manually checking a long list of tools," he says.

However, there are also challenges, particularly regarding ethics and legislation.

"We need to consider issues like copyright, the environmental impact of training models, and the risk of models hallucinating—that is, providing inaccurate information," he warns, pointing to early versions of ChatGPT as an example.

Another key challenge is public acceptance.

"It’s important to have open conversations with stakeholders to address these concerns. We need ambassadors who can also ask critical questions and understand where things might go wrong in the risk assessment process This is something we need to discuss together. It’s important to involve people in the process of all these new developments," Marc emphasizes.

Have there been any technical or scientific breakthroughs in your team so far?

Marc admits that while there haven’t been any major breakthroughs yet, the team has made progress.

"Right now, the platform is more a collection of separate components, but we have a vision of how these pieces should come together. That breakthrough is still to come," he says.

The design is already in place, but the team still needs to build the system. Collaboration with colleagues like Isaac, who is focusing on the user experience, will be helpful to achieve this.

What other new technologies or developments in this field do you find interesting with regard to the VHP?

Marc is excited about recent advancements in machine learning and how they are transforming the way we code information.

"Take AlphaFold, for instance, a model developed by Google that can predict protein structures from amino acid sequences. It’s a game-changer in chemistry," he explains.

It’s not just LLMs that are groundbreaking, Marc says—the entire AI field is evolving rapidly.

"Every week, there’s a new architecture or algorithm that improves on the previous version. It’s an exciting but also challenging time to keep up," he adds.

He also mentions the growing open science movement, that companies like Meta and Google are currently also contributing to by making their models and source code available to the public.

"This push for open science gives us much more access to knowledge than we had before," Marc says.

Have you discovered any surprising insights during your work on this project?

One key insight for Marc has been the potential of the agile working method.

"VHP4Safety is a development project, and we’re working towards a concrete product. The method we’re using to get there is agile, which has its own set of principles," he explains.

While Marc sees the potential in agile methods, he acknowledges that it can be difficult to align with the more traditional project structure, which often focuses on previously defined milestones and deliverables.

"I’d like to see more agile working in scientific projects, but that requires a transition to a new way of working. The current approach can sometimes slow down innovation, especially in fields where technology evolves so quickly," he explains.

The VHP4Safety Hackathon is coming up! What are your hopes or goals for the event?

Marc is looking forward to the Hackathon and hopes it will generate creative ideas and practical solutions.

"I hope we come away with ideas and products that we can build on immediately. That will require quite something from the participants," he admits.

He recognizes the challenge of working with diverse disciplines in a Hackathon setting but hopes participants have become more comfortable with interdisciplinary collaboration.

" The interdisciplinary aspect can be challenging, but it’s crucial to making progress," he says.

He also hopes for a positive, open atmosphere, with participants having fun while they work together.
