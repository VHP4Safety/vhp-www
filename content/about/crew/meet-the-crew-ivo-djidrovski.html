Hi Ivo! Can you tell us a little bit about yourself?
Ivo studied genetics and cell biology in Lyon, France, driven by a strong interest in stem cells. “I was really passionate about personalised and regenerative medicine,” he says.

After completing his master’s, he pursued a PhD in Newcastle as part of the in3 project, a Marie Skłodowska-Curie ITN with 14 PhD students. “Half of us worked in silico, the other half in vitro. The goal was to create a new toxicological assessment framework using iPSCs and computational tools.”

One of the innovations of in3 was to use donor cells from the same person to generate different tissues, and combine that with personalised PBPK parameters, QSARs, and read-across. “We were already doing this back in 2017,” he says. “In many ways, VHP4Safety is a logical continuation of the in3 project, which was coordinated by Paul Jennings—who is now also involved in VHP4Safety.”

After his PhD, Ivo founded a company organising online scientific events, mainly in toxicology. “It was during the pandemic and I was living in Macedonia, where my family lives, so it was nice to be close to them.” One of those events was the annual meeting of the Dutch Society of Toxicology. “That’s how I met Juliette, the coordinator of VHP4Safety. I was immediately inspired by her work and wanted to join her team.”

When did you get involved in the VHP4Safety project?
“I joined in June 2022,” Ivo says. “Juliette encouraged me to apply. The project matched my PhD experience really well, and I was hired by Raymond Pieters to work on in vitro PBPK parameters in work package 2.1.”

What is your role in the project, and how has it changed over time?
"In WP 2.1, we first considered the standard Caco-2 monolayer for generating PBPK parameters, but I preferred to work with human organoids derived from iPSCs and co-cultured with commensal bacteria so we could factor in microbiome-driven metabolism. Raymond, the work-package lead, backed the idea, and together we coordinated the wider in vitro PBPK effort and finishing the first deliverable. The work package has several researchers who are working with many different in vitro models, and I was familiar with most of those models thanks to my PhD project."

However, Ivo soon felt the urge to shift his focus. “I understood the models well, but I wanted to contribute more broadly to the platform. I was also ready to move away from lab work.” Around the same time, large language models (LLMs) became available. “I was really enthusiastic about them and wanted to explore how they could be integrated into the platform.”

The coordination team supported this shift, and since then Ivo has been more involved in tool development and the overall risk assessment strategy. “It gave me a chance to be more creative.” He also organised a hackathon on AI applications. “It helped a lot of people in the project understand how AI could support risk assessment.”

What do you find most interesting about the project?
“The challenge,” he says. “Animal-free risk assessment is not easy. The diversity of people involved makes it fascinating—everyone brings a different perspective. That makes the process slower, but also more enriching.”

You recently launched your new tool, O’QT. Can you tell us more about it?
“O’QT is a tool that uses the OECD QSAR Toolbox for hazard assessment and read-across. The tool automates the process of gathering information on new compounds. This way, you know what already exists and what still needs to be tested—potentially reducing the need for safety tests.”

Many people in the project know you as a huge fan of AI. Can you explain how the tool makes use of that?

“It uses AI agents powered by LLMs. Each agent is assigned a part of the data and has a specific expertise. Together, they simulate how a team of human risk assessors would work—discussing, drawing conclusions, and formulating a strategy. You can also access each agent’s individual strategy,” he explains. “It’s AI using our existing human-built tools. I believe this is the way forward.”
